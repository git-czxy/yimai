# Do I Still Ask?

AI is getting better and better at answering questions.

Many questions that once took a long time to research, a long time to think through, or left us not even knowing where to look for an answer can now receive a fairly good response simply by being asked.

That is, of course, a good thing.

But one day, I suddenly began to wonder:

**When answers become easier and easier to obtain, are answers still what truly matters?**

Then I asked myself three questions:

**Do I still know how to ask?**

**Do I still want to ask?**

**Am I still asking what I want to ask?**

At first, I did not find the first two questions difficult to answer.

I still know how to ask.

I still want to ask.

In fact, with AI, I can ask more than before, go deeper, and reach farther.

But when I came to the third question, I stopped.

**Am I still asking what I want to ask?**

That question was not so easy to answer.

Because a question does not arise out of nowhere.

What we have read, what we have experienced, the people around us, the content algorithms place before us—and, of course, the answers AI gives us—all of these may shape the next question.

Even who I was in the past is shaping who I am now.

So when these things come to know me better and better, become increasingly able to predict me, and even begin to organize and extend my thinking for me, how do I know:

**Is the question I am asking now still the question I truly want to ask?**

Later, I realized that the third question is itself a warning.

It does not mean that I should exclude every outside influence.

That would be neither possible nor necessary.

What may truly deserve our vigilance is the possibility that one day, I no longer even notice those influences are there.

There are more and more answers. The path becomes smoother. The system knows me better and better.

And yet the person who asks the questions slowly recedes into the background.

---

At that point, I suddenly thought of an ancient line:

**One gives birth to Two. Two gives birth to Three. Three gives birth to the ten thousand things.**

That line made me look again at the “One.”

If even that “One” is no longer there, where would the Two, the Three, and the ten thousand things come from?

AI can become the “Two.”

The world can become the “Two.”

Who I was in the past can also become the “Two.”

They enter into relation with me; we encounter, collide, and question. From this, new understandings, new questions, and new possibilities continually emerge.

Perhaps this is the “Three,” and what continues to grow from it.

But all of this seems to rest on one simple premise:

**The “One” is still there.**

And so, the three questions about asking gradually became another question:

**Am I still here?**

---

I still do not have a complete answer.

But I increasingly feel that what is worth protecting in the age of AI may not be only our knowledge, our memories, or our privacy.

There is also the person who asks questions, who doubts answers, who changes direction, and who, when faced with all the options already given, can still give a third answer.

At the very least, that person should still be there.
