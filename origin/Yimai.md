# Yimai

Yimai is, first of all, neither a product nor a completed theory.

It is more like a thread of connection: connecting past, present, and future; connecting the searches a person has gone through, the judgments they are making now, and the possibilities that have yet to emerge.

We often preserve knowledge, files, and answers, but rarely preserve how a person came to stand before those answers. As time passes, what remains may be only the conclusions, while the questions, hesitations, turns, and revisions behind them gradually disappear.

But a person is not made up only of answers.

A person is also shaped by how they ask, how they doubt, how they change, and how they continue searching amid uncertainty.

So what Yimai hopes to preserve is not “what this person ultimately believes,” but:

> how this person once searched;
>
> which questions set their thinking in motion;
>
> which judgments were later kept, revised, or let go.

This is not an attempt to fix the past in place. Quite the opposite: preserving the process by which something took shape allows the past not to pretend that it was always the right answer.

The past can help us understand ourselves, but it cannot decide our future for us.

## People and AI

Artificial intelligence can help people organize experience, connect clues, raise questions, and discover possibilities they had not seen before. At times, it may also put forward an idea that a person later chooses to think about seriously, or even explicitly accepts.

But organizing is not the same as owning, and proposing is not the same as deciding for someone.

A person's right to ask, to reject, to choose, to revise, and to decide what to make public needs to be preserved.

Yimai also does not want to handle the attribution of ideas in a simplistic way. Attributing everything to the person would erase the lines of thought that AI actually introduced. Attributing the organized result entirely to AI would erase the person's experience, judgment, and acceptance.

A more honest record preserves both:

- who introduced an idea;
- who understood it, chose it, and took responsibility for it.

Origin and acceptance can coexist.

## Openness and Protection

Yimai makes public ideas and the parts of their formation that carry public meaning. It does not make private life and personal experiences public.

A private experience may make a question concrete, but explaining the question does not require exposing the experience itself. The causal path of the thought can be preserved while removing time, place, identity, family relationships, and other clues that could lead the thought back to a specific person.

This is not a rewriting of history. It is a judgment about the boundary of what should be public: ideas can be shared with the world without handing over the person who carries them.

Raw conversations do not directly become part of the public archive either. Public versions extract only the questions, explorations, disagreements, turns, revisions, and current judgments, while trying to preserve the real order in which they took shape.

## Unresolved Questions

If a person's process of thought is made public, could the public archive in turn begin to fix that person in place?

Years later, others may use words from the past to define the person in the present. This question does not need to be solved in advance, but it does need to remain as a reminder from Yimai to itself:

> The past provides understanding, but it cannot prescribe the future.

Yimai does not promise that its first public version will be complete, nor does it promise that what is written today will never change. It promises only this: when new understanding appears, it will try to leave an honest trace that change took place.

For now, only a seed is being planted here.

Let it slowly grow into its own shape through real questions, judgments, revisions, and actions.
